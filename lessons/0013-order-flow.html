Inside the bar: order flow, lightly

Order flow is a magnifying glass on a single candle. It shows who was aggressive at each price. The book’s own verdict is that it can’t tell you why, so it only earns a look at a zone you had already marked. This lesson teaches the one pattern worth knowing, and how to see it on the daily chart you already have.

Your win today

At a trading zone, read a bar as absorption (the passive side blocking the move) or initiative (the aggressive side moving price), on a footprint and on a plain daily candle with volume. Then know what each one asks you to do next.

Why this matters to you

Your mission puts tick-level order flow out of scope “unless Deepvue or the user’s broker can show it”, and says to cover it as concepts. That matches the book. Villahermosa says order flow used on its own “could be totally meaningless” because it can’t give you the context[2.0 p.179]. He says the only time worth looking inside the candles is “when the price reaches the trading zones”[p.247]. The concepts still pay off for you. The pattern the footprint shows (absorption, then initiative) is the same pattern as a Wyckoff turn, only smaller[p.268]. Learn it once, and you can read it on daily bars.

Two stock-specific caveats. (1) In 2025, 50.6% of US stock volume traded off-exchange: wholesalers internalising retail orders, plus dark pools[Cboe]. Those prints still reach the tape, but which side was aggressive has to be inferred, so a stock’s footprint is noisier than the futures footprints in the book. (2) Tools: I couldn’t find a footprint chart in Deepvue. TradingView has a Volume Footprint chart type on its Premium plan and above[TradingView]. Check what you can actually see (homework step 1).

1 · Every trade has an aggressor

A trade happens when an aggressive order meets a passive one. Market orders are aggressive, and so are stops, because a stop becomes a market order when it triggers. Limit orders are passive: they wait in the book[pp.152–153, 168]. Passive orders can stop a move, but only aggression can move price[p.255].

The tradePrints in the…Because
Market (or triggered stop) buy meets a resting sell limitASK columnthe buyer was in a hurry and paid the ask
Market (or triggered stop) sell meets a resting buy limitBID columnthe seller was in a hurry and hit the bid

The column records who was in a hurry. It doesn’t record why. A short seller’s stop-loss is an aggressive buy, so it prints on the ASK, but nobody is opening a new long. A long’s take-profit sell limit is passive, so it lifts nothing. The book’s point is that the same action, closing a position, can print in either column depending on the order type used[pp.169–173]. Hedgers and arbitrageurs print there too, and they have no view on direction[p.179]. This is “the order-flow problem”, and it’s why every reading below starts with “potential”.

2 · Reading a footprint

A footprint splits each candle into price rows. The left number in each row is volume traded on the BID, and the right number is volume on the ASK[pp.163–164]. The bar behind the numbers is the row’s total, a profile of the candle. Under the candle: total volume, and delta (ASK volume − BID volume)[p.164]. The numbers here are shares, in hundreds.

Read it diagonally, not across. Buyers who lift the ask and sellers who hit the bid trade at two different prices. So you compare each ASK cell with the BID cell one row below it, and each BID cell with the ASK cell one row above it[pp.249–250]. An imbalance is a cell at least 3× its diagonal partner. The book suggests 200–400%, and some traders add a minimum size[p.251]. In this course it’s 300% and at least 20.

Drill 2 · Find the imbalances

Click every cell you think is an imbalance, then press Check. Hover over a cell to ring its diagonal partner. Two of the cells in the first candle look like imbalances if you read across. They aren’t.

3 · The one pattern: absorption, then initiative

The book narrows order flow down to the events that suggest “an effective market turn: potential takeover and initiative”[p.253]. That’s the three-step turn from Part 4: exhaustion → absorption → initiative[pp.150–151].

Absorption (“takeover”) big passive orders block it: heavy volume, no progress
→
Initiativebig aggressive orders move price the other way. That’s the trigger

2.0 pp.253–260. A delta divergence, such as a positive delta on a candle that fails at its high, is absorption seen from another angle: aggressive buying met by bigger passive selling (pp.175–177). It only counts in the right place.

Look back at the figure in section 2. That’s the book’s ideal bearish turn[p.259]. In bar 1, buyers lift the ask hard at the top of the bar, and the bar closes well below them: buying absorption. In bar 2, sellers hit the bid in the upper part of the bar, and it closes on its low: selling initiative, a SOW bar. Delta swings from positive to negative between them, a “change of control”.

Once the move is underway: control, then the test

A control is initiative that happens mid-move. Look for a bar with good volume, imbalances (ideally two or more), and a close in the far third of the range. Later, a test of control is a quiet, low-volume pullback to that zone, where you look for fresh initiative. That’s an entry for anyone who missed the turn[pp.262–265].

4 · The same pattern on your daily chart

The book’s last order-flow section is the one that matters most for you. It says absorption and initiative are “nothing more than a minuscule-scale representation of what in another temporality would be a cumulative or distributive scheme”[p.268].

ScaleAbsorptionInitiativeControl
Footprint (a few candles)Imbalances against the closeImbalances with the closeThe imbalance level in an impulse bar
A session or a few daysThe bell of a P or b profile[L11]The breakout from that valueThe VPOC of a profile over the impulse leg
Your daily structureThe range itself: SC/ST/Spring bars with heavy volume and no progress[L3]The SOS / SOW bar, then the trend[L6]An HVN. The LPS / BUEC is its test

So on a daily candle: absorption = high volume, narrow spread, close away from the push, right at your zone. Initiative = wide range, high volume, close at the extreme. The control of a whole impulse leg is the VPOC of a fixed-range profile drawn over that leg, and the book suggests looking for the next entry when price comes back to it[pp.269–270]. You lose the inside view, and you enter a little later. The book says you reach the same conclusion “albeit with less stress”[p.178].

Real charts · Do it in Deepvue (~20 min)

  1. Tool check (2 min). In Deepvue and in your broker, look for a footprint chart, Time & Sales (the tape), or a delta indicator. Note what exists. Also note what Deepvue’s indicator search shows for “volume profile” or “VBP”. That question has been open since Lesson 11.
  2. Pick one watchlist stock that is sitting at, or just turned from, a zone you’d marked (a range low or high, a VAL/VAH, an LPS area). On the daily chart, find the absorption bar (heavy volume, narrow spread, close away from the push) and the initiative bar (wide, heavy, closing at the extreme). If there’s no initiative bar yet, say so. Then there’s no trigger.
  3. If an impulse has already started, draw a fixed-range profile over the leg from the initiative bar to the leg’s extreme. Its VPOC is the control. Write down the plan for a test of it: entry trigger, stop beyond the leg’s LVN or origin, and target.
