Is this trend healthy or tiring?

Lesson 1 told you whether a stock is trending. This one tells you how much life the trend has left.

Your win today

Look at the last three swings of any trend and give a one-line verdict: healthy, or tiring, and here’s the evidence. Then draw a channel on it that tells you where the next pullback should hold.

Why this matters to a stock trader

Every range from Lesson 1 starts as a trend that runs out of steam. If you can see the steam running out, you stop adding to a long just before the stock goes sideways for three months, and you start watching for the range to form.

Villahermosa’s rule for the whole chapter: “Our job is to go for strength and against weakness.” And the method is always comparison: “Absolute values are not sought. It is a question of comparing the current movements with the previous ones.”[Classic p.20]

The one rule to keep

“A weakness in the price does not imply a change of trend, it is simply a sign of loss of strength.”[Classic p.20] Weakness is an alert. It is not a sell signal.

Two kinds of wave

A trend moves in impulses (waves in the trend’s direction) separated by pullbacks (waves against it)[Classic p.17]. Number the swing points and you can compare them:

Three comparisons

ToolCompareStrength (uptrend)Weakness (uptrend)
Speed[p.21]The angle of this impulse vs. the last oneSteeper or the sameFlatter: it takes more bars to go the same distance
Projection[pp.22–23]How far this impulse travels vs. the last one, and how far the new high clears the old oneLonger or the sameShorter. If it can’t make a new high at all, “the movement may be reaching its end”
Depth[pp.23–24]How deep this pullback goes vs. the last oneShallower or the sameDeeper: sellers are getting bolder

In a downtrend, flip everything: impulses are the falls and pullbacks are the bounces. Bigger bounces and shorter falls mean the downtrend is tiring, which is how accumulation usually begins.

Lines: the trend’s stride

Lines make those comparisons visible at a glance[Classic p.24]. Bruce Fraser calls the trend line “the stride, at which the price will be moving for the foreseeable future”[Fraser].

  1. Demand line (uptrend): join two rising swing lows. It’s where buyers are expected to show up[p.26]. In a downtrend, the supply line joins two falling swing highs.
  2. Overbought line: copy the demand line parallel through the high between the two lows. Together they make the channel[p.28]. In a downtrend, copy the supply line through the low between the highs to get the oversold line.
  3. More touches mean a more reliable line. A line that’s too steep “will be broken too soon”. Don’t draw lines on every minor wiggle[pp.24, 26].

Reading the channel

What price doesWhat it suggests
Pullbacks hold the demand line, rallies reach the overbought lineHealthy. “Appropriate to consider maintaining or adding positions”[p.26]
A rally fails to reach the overbought lineWeakness. It warns of a possible turn[p.31]. Fraser reads the same failure in a downtrend as the index being “tired of declining”[Fraser]
Price shoots above the overbought lineOverbought. Acceleration that’s too fast makes the stock sensitive to profit-taking, and it usually leads to a correction[p.28]
Price breaks the demand lineAn alert, not a verdict. “What is significant is how the line is broken.” Often the trend just pauses in a range and resumes at a new angle[p.27]

The healthy-or-tiring read

  1. Number the last three impulses and two pullbacks.
  2. Projection: are the impulses getting shorter? Depth: are the pullbacks getting deeper? Speed: is the angle flattening?
  3. Draw the channel and ask: did the last rally reach the top line, and did the last pullback hold the bottom one?
  4. Verdict in one line: “healthy” or “tiring, because …”. If it’s tiring, start watching for a range. Don’t short it yet.

Real charts · Do it in Deepvue (~25 min)

  1. From your Lesson 1 list, take three stocks you called “uptrend” (or any three in clear daily uptrends) and one in a clear downtrend.
  2. On the daily chart, mark the last three impulses and two pullbacks. Use the measure tool, or read the prices off, to get each one’s size in % and in bars.
  3. Draw the demand line with the trend-line tool through two swing lows (two swing highs for the downtrend). Then draw the parallel line through the extreme between them. A parallel-channel tool does this in one step, if your toolbar has one.
  4. Give each stock a verdict and paste it back to me like this. I’ll check your swing numbers against the price data: 
    NVDA — impulses +18% / +12% / +7%, pullbacks −5% / −6%
       — demand line from Aug 5 low to Sep 6 low, last rally missed overbought line
       — TIRING (projection shrinking, failed to reach top of channel)
Primary source: read this next (~20 min)

Villahermosa, The Wyckoff Methodology in Depth (your Classic Wyckoff.pdf). Read Ch. 3 “Trends” and Ch. 4 “Assessing trends” (PDF pp.17–32). The diagrams on pp.22–24 number the swings the same way this lesson does.

Cross-check: Bruce Fraser, “Trendapalooza” (StockCharts, 2015), on supply/demand lines, overbought/oversold lines and “stride”. Also see “S&P 500 Trending” (2019), where a failure to return to the overbought line is the warning.

Stuck? Ask me

If a real chart’s swings are messy and you can’t tell which lows to anchor on, paste the ticker and the date range. Choosing anchors is a judgement call, and it’s easier to learn by arguing about a real example.
