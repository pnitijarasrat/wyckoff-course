Read the traces, not the labels: sloping ranges

Real ranges are rarely flat, and their events are rarely textbook-clean. Wyckoff 2.0 opens by loosening the rules from Lessons 4–8. It gives you two traces that still work when the labels don’t fit: the Shortening of the Thrust and the structural failure.

Your win today

Given a range that tilts up or down, draw its channel, measure whether the thrusts are shortening, check for a structural failure, and name which of the four sloping structures it is. That tells you which way it’s likely to break.

Why this matters to you

Your mission says you should label events “without forcing labels.” On your Deepvue watchlist, most ranges won’t be flat boxes. A stock in a strong group often builds a range that drifts up, and a weak one drifts down. If you only look for horizontal ranges, you’ll skip those stocks or mislabel them. The book says the method is “much richer” than labelling a chart “almost robotically”[2.0 p.13].

1 · The labels serve the read

Two structures are almost never identical, so most of the time the events will be “more complex” to identify than the classic examples[p.13]. The book’s rule: what matters is “the action as a whole.” If you can’t find a clean SC, AR and ST, but the trend has objectively stopped and turned sideways, don’t discard the stock. “This is a mistake”[p.14]. Look instead for what is objective, for example a level price failed at several times and then broke (the Creek). “This is the key.”

So the question changes from “which label goes here?” to “who is gaining control?”, judged from the traces[p.16]. You already did this with the tally in Lesson 7. Today adds two more traces to it.

2 · Structural failure: missing the other edge

First find the structure price is respecting: two edges (supply above, demand below) that it keeps turning at. The more touches, the more you can trust it. Then assume it carries on moving from edge to edge[p.31].

A structural failure is when price turns before reaching the opposite edge[pp.31–34]:

What you seeRead
▼ WeaknessA rally that can’t reach the top of the structureBuyers are losing control; sellers are showing up
▲ StrengthA decline that can’t reach the bottomBuyers are “blocking the price from falling”. They don’t want anyone else to get on board

You’ve seen this before: an LPS or LPSY is a structural failure, the point where price can’t get back to the shakeout and breaks out from the middle instead[p.32]. Two cautions from the book. It’s “one more element” of the context, not a reversal signal on its own[pp.33–34]. And price moving from edge to edge is just the structure working. That’s not a trace, so don’t score it.

3 · Shortening of the Thrust (SOT)

A tool Wyckoff used to measure exhaustion: “each new extreme travels a shorter distance than the previous extreme”[p.35]. It’s Lesson 2’s projection, measured across several pushes in a row. In a fall, measure how far each new low goes below the one before. In a rise, how far each new high goes above.

  • You need three thrusts at least. Start looking after three or four pushes[p.36].
  • Check the volume on the short thrust. Heavy volume means effort without result (Lesson 3): the other side is absorbing. Light volume means the trend side is running out[p.36].
  • More than four pushes, still shortening? The trend “may be too strong to trade against”[p.36].
  • Confirmation is a strong move the other way on high volume. Then wait for a pullback and join it[p.36].
  • Context comes first. If price just made an Upthrust, a bullish SOT on the way down is a trap: the shakeout sets the bias. The same goes for a bearish SOT after a Spring[p.37].

4 · Sloping ranges: the same range, tilted

A sloping structure develops “in exactly the same way” as a flat one: same events, same phases. Tilt the chart in your head until it’s level, and the story is identical[pp.39–40]. The slope only tells you who had more control to start with: up = buyers, down = sellers.

How to draw it. Start with horizontal edges from Phase A. If price in Phase B keeps leaving them, try a slope: connect the highs or lows of Phases A–C, and copy the line to the other side. It doesn’t need to be exact, and it can be a zone rather than a line. It should “jump out” and contain almost all the price action. Keep the horizontal levels too, because price may come back to them[pp.40–41].

The four kinds. The slope alone doesn’t tell you the outcome. SOT and structural failure do[pp.42–56]:

StructureConditionThe traces that give it away
Accumulation, sloping upStrongestRising highs and lows, with buyers defending every dip. A probe above a high looks like an Upthrust, but then price “fails to even reach the bottom” of the channel: a structural failure of strength. It’s hard to buy because price always looks high.
Accumulation, sloping downWeakest accumulationFalling highs and lows, but the new lows travel less each time (SOT). A pullback then finds support in the middle (structural failure). Confirmed by a strong break of the channel; buy the pullback.
Distribution, sloping downWeakestFalling highs and lows with no SOT and no structural failure. Heavy volume on the drops, then a UTAD (an overshoot of the top, or of a previous high). Moves fast. Hard to short because price always looks low.
Distribution, sloping upStrength fadingRising highs, but each new high gains less (SOT). A rally then can’t reach the top (structural failure of weakness), often after a UTAD on heavy volume.

Pages: up-sloping accumulation pp.42–45, down-sloping accumulation pp.45–49, down-sloping distribution pp.49–52, up-sloping distribution pp.53–56. The book’s examples also use Volume Profile and VWAP levels; those come in Lesson 11.

Drill 1 · Draw the channel

Two ranges, cut before the outcome. Click two swing points on one edge. The tool copies your line to the other side through the extreme between them. You’ll find out how both charts end later in the lesson.

Drill 2 · Read the traces, step by step

Two ranges with the same down-slope after the same kind of fall. The chart reveals one trace at a time. Score each as strength, weakness or no clear sign, then name the structure. The first chart is the one you just drew.

Drill 3 · Quick decisions

Questions 1 and 2 are both up-sloping ranges after a rally. H = swing high, L = swing low. Measure the thrusts and check the last swing against the channel.

Real charts · Do it in Deepvue (~25 min)

  1. Scroll your watchlist on the daily chart and find one range that tilts: up or down, at least a few weeks long. Strong groups give up-sloping ones; laggards give down-sloping ones.
  2. Draw the channel: two trend lines, one on the Phase A–C highs and one parallel on the lows. Draw zones if the touches are ragged.
  3. SOT: write down how far each of the last three or four new highs (or new lows) travelled past the one before. Shrinking? Note the volume on the shortest one.
  4. Structural failure: did the most recent swing reach the opposite edge, or turn early?
  5. Name it as one of the four kinds, and say what would confirm it (a strong break on volume, then a pullback). Add your Lesson 8 plan if it’s tradeable.
  6. Paste it back like this, and I’ll check it against the daily data: 
    XYZ — daily, after a fall. Range sloping down since Aug 4
      Lows: $48.20 → $46.10 (−2.1) → $45.00 (−1.1) → $44.60 (−0.4), volume falling = SOT
      Last pullback held $46.50, channel bottom ~$44 = structural failure (strength)
      Call: accumulation sloping down. Confirm: close above the channel top ~$49 on volume
      Plan: buy the pullback after that; stop under $46.50
Primary source: read this next (~45 min)

Villahermosa, Wyckoff 2.0 (your Wyckoff 2.0.pdf), Part 1 “Advanced concepts”: §1.1 “The labels” (PDF pp.13–16), §1.5 “Structural failure” (pp.31–34), §1.6 “Shortening of the Thrust” (pp.35–37) and §1.7.1 “Sloping structures” (pp.38–56). Look at every example chart in §1.7.1 and try tilting it level in your head. Also read §1.4 “Accumulation or failed distribution” (pp.24–30). It explains why a range that has every bullish trace can still fail at the BUEC, and why you trade in the direction of the last shakeout. For more on SOT, David Weis’s Trades About to Happen (2013) is the standard modern treatment.
