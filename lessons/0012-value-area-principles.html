Inside, outside, back in: trading with value areas

Lesson 11 gave you the levels: VPOC, VAH, VAL. This lesson gives you the rules for what to do when price reaches them. There are four. They all start from the same question: where is price relative to the value area, and when it tries to get in, does it succeed?

Your win today

Look at any stock against a reference value area and name the play. That means which of the four principles applies, which side you trade, where the trigger is, where the target is, and what would cancel it.

Why this matters to you

Two of your mission goals meet here: “build bullish and bearish scenarios… and mark trading zones in advance”, and “add Volume Profile… to find levels more objectively.” The book says these principles are universal. They work the same on the value area of “a candlestick, session, movement or structure”[2.0 p.236], so they apply to the daily ranges you trade. They also make you hold both scenarios at once: the same VAL can be a buy on Monday and a short on Friday. What decides it is what price does at the VAL[p.240].

Two rules apply to all four. Manage at the first relevant level. The book says a buy at the VAL “should necessarily be managed on the VPOC”[p.245]. So when price reaches the VPOC, you bank part of the position or tighten the stop. You don’t just hope for the far edge. Confluence beats a lone level. The trades that deserve most confidence have a trigger where several levels line up, such as a VAL that is also the weekly VWAP or an older VPOC[p.238].

About the “80%”. The book calls reversion an “adaptation of Market Profile’s 80% rule”[p.239]. The original is intraday: price opens outside the value area, then trades back inside for two consecutive 30-minute periods, and that is said to give an “80% chance” of crossing the whole value area. A third-party summary traces it to Dalton Capital Management’s Profile Reports (1987–91)[marketcalls]. Nobody publishes an audited statistic for it. One trader’s anecdotal backtest, posted on a forum, got closer to 62%. Treat “80%” as the rule’s name, not as your odds.

3 · Translating it to daily stock charts

The book’s examples are intraday futures, using the previous session’s profile. It says longer-term traders should use “weekly profiles or Composite-type profiles that include more price action”[p.237]. For you, the steps are these:

  • Reference profile: the fixed-range profile over the current Wyckoff range (Lesson 11), from the stopping action to the latest bar before the move you’re judging. When there’s no clear range, use the previous month.
  • “Opens away from value” in the book becomes a gap outside the value area. It’s October, which means earnings season, so you’ll see plenty of them this month.
  • Acceptance back inside (my working rule, not the book’s): at least two daily closes inside the value area, and the first pullback holds the edge from the inside. One close inside is only an attempt. This is Lesson 11’s price + time + volume test, in daily bars.

Real charts · Do it in Deepvue (~20 min)

  1. Take the range you profiled for Lesson 11. If you didn’t do that one, pick any watchlist stock that has been in a range for 1–3 months. Pull the fixed-range profile over the range and write down its VAL, VPOC and VAH.
  2. Where is price now: inside, outside, or on its way back in? Name the principle, then write the trade the way the drills did: side, trigger level, minimum target, full target, and what cancels it.
  3. Earnings-gap watch. Find one stock on your watchlist that gaps outside its value area this week. Write your call before the next session opens: reversion or continuation? Then check it after 5 sessions. This is the fastest feedback loop you have this month.
