Phases D and E: the breakout and its test

The shakeout is done. Now price runs to the edge of the range and breaks out. This lesson is about not buying the breakout itself, and buying the quiet pullback that follows it instead.

Your win today

After a Phase C shakeout, label the SOS/JAC that breaks the Creek, the LPS and BUEC that test it, and the first Phase E pullbacks. Then, when price has just broken out, decide whether to buy the pullback, wait, or call it a failed break.

Why this matters to you

You already trade breakouts, so this is the lesson that changes how you trade them. The book says the breakout “is not an opportunity to trade; it only alerts us to a possible opportunity in the very near future”. The opportunity is the test that comes after it[Classic p.154]. That test was “the preferred position for Richard Wyckoff to enter the market”, because by then all the evidence of the campaign is on your left[p.170]. Your stop goes under that test and under the broken Creek[p.171].

Two levels: the Creek and the Ice

The Creek is the resistance at the top of an accumulation range, set by the AR high and the Phase B highs. The Ice is the support at the bottom of a distribution range, set by the AR low and the Phase B lows[pp.38, 42]. The image is Robert Evans’s: price takes a run and jumps across the creek, then steps back to its edge to check that the far bank holds[ChartSchool]. The Creek is “the last barrier that must be overcome to conclude that one side has final control”[p.187].

Phase D: the trend inside the range

Phase D starts once the Phase C test is over and lasts until the breakout has been confirmed[p.186]. It has two events:

EventAccumulationDistributionWhat you want to see
BreakSOS Sign of Strength, also called JAC Jump Across the CreekMSOW Major Sign of Weakness, which breaks the IceStarts at the Phase C extreme. Wide bars, rising volume, and it moves easily through earlier highs[pp.155, 161, 164]
ConfirmationLPS Last Point of Support, also called BUEC Back Up to the Edge of the CreekLPSY Last Point of Supply, also called FTI Fall Through the IceA pullback to the broken level on narrow, overlapping bars and low volume, which stays outside the range[pp.167–168]

A strong rally after the Spring that gets close to the Creek but doesn’t break it is a minor SOS (mSOS)[p.162]. The book gives three outcomes when price reaches the Creek[p.187]:

  1. Pull back to an LPS inside the range, then attack again. The book compares this to “taking a run to jump the river.”
  2. Break straight through. The large traders absorb all the sell orders at the Creek, even though it costs them a worse price.
  3. Fail, and turn into a new shakeout the other way. If the structure has developed as expected, this is the least likely outcome, but keep it in mind.

There are three kinds of LPS

The LPS is a higher low, where “buyers appear to stop the fall” before the next push[p.174]. Its name depends on where it appears:

  • After the shakeout: the Test of the Spring is itself an LPS.
  • Inside the range: the pullback after an SOS or mSOS that hasn’t yet left the range.
  • Outside the range: the BUEC, and then every pullback in the Phase E uptrend.

“Many traders… will be buying during the development of the strength signal (SOS). But this action is not correct, it is best to wait for the next reaction (LPS)”[pp.174–175]. An LPS sometimes forms at the level where the PS appeared, because that’s where the large traders began buying[p.175].

Real break or false break? Read the pullback

When price breaks out, you can’t tell yet whether it’s an SOS or an upthrust. The pullback after it tells you[pp.157–160, 168–170]:

ClueGenuine break (BUEC)Failing break (UT)
Re-entryPrice stays outside the range. This is “the most reliable sign of intentionality”Price falls back into the range
Pullback barsNarrow and overlapping, on less volume than the breakWide bars on heavy volume
DistanceThe break carried well clear of the rangeThe break barely cleared the level
The in-between case: a heavy pullback that still holds

“If there is a relatively high volume on the confirmation test, it is best to proceed with caution… we should wait for successive tests to be developed over the zone.”[p.169] If the pullback is both wide and heavy, the break has probably failed: “most likely at this point the price will re-enter the range leaving the potential break finally as a shaking”[p.170].

Two more notes. A breakout on only moderate volume isn’t automatically bad. It can mean there are simply very few sellers left[pp.156–157]. And a structure can do everything right and still fail at the BUEC. If price fails at the BUEC and sellers take over, the range was distribution all along[2.0 p.26]. That’s why you keep the stop under the broken Creek.

Phase E: the trend outside the range

Phase E starts after the confirmation. From here, one side is in control and “we should only seek to trade in that direction”[p.189]. It’s a series of SOS and LPS moves, or SOW and LPSY moves in a downtrend. “From this point on… we must implement all the tools for evaluating trends”[p.190]. Those are the speed, projection and depth checks from Lesson 2. In Phase E, the second book looks for small accumulation ranges inside the trend, and uses them as places to join it[2.0 p.94].

The Phase D–E read

  1. Creek or Ice: draw it through the AR and the Phase B extremes before price gets there.
  2. Strength or weakness: from the Phase C low, look for wide bars and rising volume that move easily and reach at least the middle of the range. Pullbacks should stay above the Spring low[p.161].
  3. At the Creek: either it breaks (SOS/JAC), or it stalls (mSOS). If it stalls, watch for an LPS inside the range.
  4. After the break, don’t chase. Wait for the pullback.
  5. Grade the pullback. Quiet and holding means a BUEC, and you can act. Heavy but holding means wait for another test. Back inside the range means it was an UT.
  6. Phase E: trade only in the direction of the break. Each LPS is a place to add, and the trend-health tools tell you when the trend is getting tired.

Real charts · Do it in Deepvue (~25 min)

  1. Use the range from your Lesson 5 homework that has already broken out. If you didn’t do it, find a base on your watchlist that broke out in the last few months.
  2. Draw the Creek through the AR high and the Phase B highs. (For a breakdown, draw the Ice.)
  3. Mark the SOS/JAC, and any mSOS and inside-range LPS before it. Then mark the BUEC and the first two Phase E LPS points.
  4. Grade the BUEC. Compare its volume with the breakout’s volume and with the 50-day average. Did it close back inside the range at any point?
  5. Live: scan your watchlist for one stock that is right now pulling back to a Creek it broke recently. Write down whether it’s quiet or heavy, and what you’d need to see before you buy it.

Primary source: read this next (~25 min)

Villahermosa, The Wyckoff Methodology in Depth (your Classic Wyckoff.pdf). Read Chapter 19, “Breakout” (PDF pp.154–166), then Chapter 20, “Confirmation” (pp.167–177). Together they’re the textbook version of “don’t chase, buy the test.” Finish with the one-page summaries of Phase D (pp.186–188) and Phase E (pp.189–190). For the vocabulary, see the event list under the schematics (pp.38–42).

Second book: Villahermosa, Wyckoff 2.0, PDF p.26 (a failed structure: every clue bullish until it fails at the BUEC), pp.68, 87–88 (planning one move at a time: Spring → breakout → BUEC) and p.94 (small re-accumulation ranges at the BUEC and in Phase E).

Cross-check: Bogomazov & Lipsett, “The Wyckoff Method: A Tutorial” (StockCharts ChartSchool). It defines SOS, LPS, BU and LPSY, explains Evans’s “jump across the creek”, and calls the back-up “both short-term profit-taking and a test for additional supply.”
