Case study: the top that wasn’t

Lesson 17 was a top that broke. This one looked just like it: a climax, new highs on light volume, a fall back to the Ice, then a break under it. And then it went up 40%. The skill isn’t calling it on the day of the break. It’s knowing what to watch for after it.

Your win today

Weigh a real range trace by trace, and tell a Spring from an MSOW by what happens after the break. Then turn a stop-out into a Phase C buy.

Why this matters to you

Your mission asks you to tell re-accumulation from distribution. Lesson 7 called it the hardest call, and the book agrees: it’s “one of the most compromising situations that any Wyckoff trader will encounter”[Classic p.79]. Bruce Fraser adds the trap for holders: re-accumulations “can take months to form”, so traders often sell them too early[Fraser 2022]. Lesson 17 taught you to get out of a top. This lesson covers the other half: how to get back in when the “top” turns out to be a shakeout.

1 · The same break, two endings

Both stocks closed under the Ice after a range that leaned weak. Here is how each one went on from there:

Lesson 17: a real topToday: a Spring
Volume on the break1.7–2.2×, with a gap down after a report0.9–1.3×. More than the quiet weeks before, but not heavy
The rally back to the IceOpened above it, closed back under (the LPSY)Closed back above it, back inside the range
The pullback after thatA new low the next dayA test on the lightest volume since the holidays, above the low

The first row doesn’t settle it. The book allows a Spring with “an increase in both volume and price ranges” (its Spring #2), as long as a test follows[Classic pp.146, 149]. The second row does. Wyckoff 2.0 calls a shakeout with a re-entry into the range “the dominant event”, and it gives “a higher relevance to the latest information”[2.0 p.78].

The holder’s rule, second half (mine, the follow-on to Lesson 17’s): a close under the Ice gets you out. A close back above it, then a quieter test that holds over the break’s low, gets you back in, with a stop under that low. Selling under the Ice costs you a few dollars when it was a Spring. Holding through a real MSOW costs a lot more.

Context check (Lesson 10). The whole market made its low on the same day as this Spring: the Nasdaq-100 ETF (QQQ) fell about 10% from February into that day, then turned. Many Springs in single stocks happen on a market-wide flush. So check the index before you call a stock’s break its own.

How the case works. As in Lessons 16 and 17: no ticker and no dates until the end, and volume is shown as a multiple of the stock’s 50-session median. The axis fits only the bars you can see.

Drill 1 · Weigh the range, trace by trace

Seven traces, from the climax to the test. Score each one as it looked that day, even if you suspect the ending. Then make the call.

Drill 2 · Plan the Phase C long

Same moment: the close of the second test day. Buy at the close. Then place the stop and the target yourself, and size it for a $25,000 account at 1% risk.

Drill 3 · Recall: today and earlier

The last three cards come from Lessons 17, 16 and 12.

Real charts · Do it in Deepvue (~20 min)

  1. Find one stock that broke under the low of a sideways range in the last two months. A stock you sold on that break is the best kind. Your watchlist is fine, and so is any big name that fell with the market.
  2. Write five lines: 
    XYZ (daily)

Break: first close under it on date · volume ___×
