Analyse a chart from zero: top down

Until now every drill handed you a range that was already in view. Real life starts with a blank ticker. Today’s routine: start on the weekly, decide whether there’s anything to do, and only then drop to the daily to look for a small structure that fits inside the big one.

Your win today

Open any stock cold and, in a few minutes, give it one of four verdicts: wait for a trend stop, wait for a range extreme, skip it, or drop to the daily. When it’s the last one, you’ll know which minor structure you’re looking for, and which one would flip you to the other side.

Why this matters to you

Your mission’s first success test is “given any US stock chart in Deepvue, I can say where it sits in the price cycle.” Any chart, not one that’s been picked out for you. The book calls starting from a blank chart “one of the first barriers” for beginners[2.0 p.89]. You have a whole watchlist to get through and about an hour a day, so you need a fast way to throw out most tickers and go deep on a few.

1 · Clean chart, start high

Two rules come first. Keep the chart clean. Once a structure has finished, delete its labels and keep only its levels, so old drawings don’t bias the new read[p.89]. Start on the higher timeframe and work down. Which one? “Whichever one is necessary.” Usually the weekly already shows everything relevant, and you won’t need the monthly[p.89].

On the weekly, look for the stopping action of the last trend and the sideways movement after it. What you want to find is a market building a cause: a range in Phase B[pp.89–90]. That gives four verdicts:

What the weekly showsVerdict
A trend still running, with no stopping action yetWait for a trend stop. Nothing to do until a change of character starts Phase A[p.90].
A range in Phase B, price in the middleWait for a range extreme. The breakout or shakeout happens at the edges[p.91].
A range with cause built, price at an edge: a potential breakout or shakeoutDrop to the daily. “This is the ideal context to move down the time frame”[p.90].
No clear trend, and no range that follows a stopSkip it. First try a higher timeframe. If nothing’s clear there either, move on to another stock[pp.101–102].

The full context also includes the trading zones above and below price, as likely targets. The book finds those with Volume Profile, which starts in Lesson 11[pp.92–93]. For now, use what you learned in Lesson 8: the range edges and the old swing highs and lows.

An even higher “timeframe” for a stock is the market itself. Wyckoff’s five-step approach starts with the trend of the overall market, and only then picks stocks that are moving with it[ChartSchool].

2 · Drop down: minor structures inside major ones

When the weekly is at an edge, you go to the daily for one reason: to find a minor structure that plays the role the weekly is waiting for[pp.93–95]. The weekly position tells you which one to look for:

Weekly positionLook on the daily for…
Potential Spring (Phase C)A minor accumulation. If it’s confirmed, the whole minor structure is the weekly Spring[p.94].
Potential BUEC (Phase D)A minor re-accumulation. It acts as the test of the broken weekly range[p.94].
Markup (Phase E)Fast minor accumulations to join the trend. When the move is urgent, these are quick[pp.94–95].

For shorts, flip each row: a potential UTAD with a minor distribution, and so on.

Every edge cuts both ways

“The situation of potential Spring is also at the same time a situation of potential effective bearish breakout.” If the daily shows strong selling instead of a minor accumulation, drop the long and switch to the bearish scenario. The same goes for a potential breakout: a minor distribution there makes it an Upthrust[pp.95–96]. That’s why the book says to “always” have both a long and a short scenario ready[p.96].

Three more rules:

  • Larger beats smaller. When the two disagree, give the bigger structure more weight. You can go further down, but “the lower you go, the more noise you will observe”[p.96].
  • Plan only the next move. In Phase B, you don’t yet know whether it’s accumulation or distribution. You plan the next swing, and the one after it once that’s done[p.68].
  • A line isn’t a trade. Price touching the weekly support or a channel line gives you a location. It isn’t a reason to buy. Wait for a minor structure there[pp.63–65].

For your stocks: the book’s futures examples go from monthly, weekly or daily down to 8, 4 or 2-hour charts[p.93]. For swing trading stocks, weekly → daily is the natural pair. Use the hourly chart only to time the entry, if you want to.

3 · Going up: minor events become major ones

This works the other way too. When you’re first identifying a structure, the small timeframe often shows it first[p.97]. Say a stock is rising in Phase E and the daily builds a distribution. On the weekly, that distribution is the Buying Climax, and the fall it causes is the weekly Automatic Reaction[pp.97–98]. So a minor distribution after a long markup doesn’t start markdown yet. It starts Phase A of a weekly range, and you’re back at the top of the routine.

A minor accumulation can also fail and become part of a bigger one. If the BUEC fails, step back and read all of it as one larger structure. The minor JAC becomes just a test of strength in the bigger range[p.100].

Real charts · Do it in Deepvue (~25 min)

  1. Take five tickers from your watchlist. Clear the old drawings first, but keep the levels.
  2. Weekly, about two minutes each. Give each one a verdict: wait for a trend stop, wait for a range extreme, skip, or drop down. Write one line of evidence (“SC Mar ’26 on 3× volume, Phase B since, price mid-range”).
  3. For each drop-down (if none of the five qualify, add tickers until one does): go to the daily. Which minor structure are you looking for (from the table in section 2)? Is it forming, or is the opposite one?
  4. Write both scenarios for that stock, each with the level that would trigger it.
  5. Paste it back like this, and I’ll check it against the real weekly and daily data: 
    NVDA: wait for range extreme (weekly range $X–$Y since …, price mid)
XYZ: drop down. Weekly: Phase B since Jun, price at the range bottom ($41) = potential Spring
     Daily: minor SC $40.10 on 2.5× volume, AR $43.20, ST $40.60 on light volume … forming accumulation
     Long: buy the LPS after an mSOS above $43.20, stop under $40.10, target the weekly top ~$49
     Short: daily closes below $40 and can't get back over it → weekly breakdown, short the LPSY
ABC: skip (no stop on weekly; messy daily)
Primary source: read this next (~40 min)

Villahermosa, Wyckoff 2.0 (your Wyckoff 2.0.pdf), Part 2 “Resolving frequently asked questions”: §2.4 “How to analyze a chart from 0?” (PDF pp.89–100) is today’s core, including the real example on p.100 of a failed minor accumulation that turns into a larger one. Then §2.5 “What to do when the context is not clear?” and “The controller” (pp.101–103). If you have time, also read §2.1 “Efficient use of lines” (pp.62–65) and §2.2 “Label changes and scenario building” (pp.66–69). They’re short, and they explain why a line is a location and not a signal.
