Volume at price: the auction and the profile

Up to now, volume has been the bars under the chart: when the big players traded. The Volume Profile turns those bars on their side and shows where they traded. A Wyckoff range is an auction, and the profile shows you the prices that auction settled on.

Your win today

Pull a profile over a Wyckoff range and read five levels off it: VPOC, VAH, VAL, HVN and LVN. Then use them: where you wait for the BUEC or LPSY, who is in control, where the stop goes, and which target price is likely to reach.

Why this matters to you

Your mission’s last success test is “I can add Volume Profile (POC, value area) to that analysis to find levels more objectively.” Lesson 8 put targets at old highs and lows, which you picked by eye. The book’s argument is that the profile is “totally objective as it does not require any interpretation”. The levels are simply where the shares changed hands[2.0 p.180]. Real structures are messy (“not as genuine as shown in the ideal book examples”), and the profile still gives you levels to work with when the events are hard to read[pp.217–218].

1 · The market is an auction

The book builds on Auction Market Theory (Steidlmayer, who designed the Market Profile for the Chicago Board of Trade)[p.180]. Three variables decide where value is[pp.133–135]:

VariableIts job in the auction
PriceExplores. It moves to new levels to see whether anyone wants to trade there.
TimeShows acceptance. Price spends little time at levels that are a bargain for one side, and a lot of time where both sides are comfortable.
VolumeConfirms it. The more shares change hands at a level, the more value the market gives it.

The market switches between two states: horizontal (balance: buyers and sellers agree, which is a range) and vertical (imbalance: price searches for a new agreement, which is a trend)[pp.135–137]. When price leaves a range, watch for one of two outcomes:

  • Acceptance: price holds out there, spends time, and volume builds. A new value area is forming.
  • Rejection: price snaps back into the old range quickly, with a sharp turn[p.137].

“All horizontal developments end when there is no longer agreement… all vertical developments end when the price reaches a zone where there is agreement again.”[p.137] Steidlmayer’s four steps (trend → stop → sideways → transition) are, in the book’s words, “essentially exactly” Phases A to E[pp.138–141]. You already know this cycle. The profile adds a measurement.

2 · Anatomy of a profile

A profile is a sideways histogram: one bar for each price level, and its length is the volume traded there. The ordinary volume bars tell you when the big players were active. The profile tells you where[pp.202–203].

LevelWhat it isHow you use it
VPOC Volume Point of ControlThe single price with the most volume: the fairest price, where the big players built most of their position[p.189].A magnet, so avoid opening trades right at it. Price above it means buyers are in control. Price below it means sellers are[p.190].
Value Area VAH / VALThe band around the VPOC that holds ~70% of the volume. The book uses 68.2%, one standard deviation. VAH = its top, VAL = its bottom[pp.185–186].The area of acceptance. Its edges act as support and resistance[p.186].
HVN High Volume NodeA peak: an old balance area where both sides were comfortable trading[pp.192–193].A magnet: price tends to get pulled back to it. Targets go here[pp.193, 235].
LVN Low Volume NodeA valley: prices neither side wanted to trade at[p.193].A rejection zone. Price either V-turns off it or runs through it fast. Good for entries, and stops go beyond it[pp.193–195, 235].

Two more ideas. The edges of a Wyckoff range are LVNs by nature: those turns are where price refused to trade[p.215]. And the market’s memory is short. The most recent balance area is the one price looks for first, and older ones matter less the longer price has been away[pp.182–183].

Finished or unfinished auction. The very top or bottom of a profile can taper off thinly. That’s a finished auction: interest faded, so it’s a rejection. Or it can end abruptly in a fat node. That’s unfinished: price will likely come back to it. If you’re planning a breakout up and the bottom of the range is unfinished, put the long scenario on hold. A visit down there probably comes first. When in doubt, treat it as finished[pp.186–188].

3 · Which profile, and where to anchor it

TypeCoversYour use (swing trading stocks)
Fixed rangeWhatever bars you selectThe workhorse. Pull it over the structure, “from the time the rotation starts until just before the imbalance”. Leave out the breakout and the test, because those are what you’re trading[pp.196–198, 217].
CompositeA long period (months or years), or everything on screenContext: the HVNs and LVNs above and below price. That’s where targets come from[pp.200–201].
SessionOne dayIntraday trading. Skip it for now[pp.198–200].

The book’s examples use futures, with intraday data. On daily stock charts, a fixed range over the structure plus a composite of 1–2 years covers almost everything. The book’s companion level is the weekly VWAP (the average price paid this week, weighted by volume). Institutions judge their fills against it, so price above both the VWAP and the VPOC means buyers are in control, and price below both means sellers are[pp.190–192, 221–222]. Deepvue has VWAP.

4 · Putting it on a Wyckoff structure

Where the test after the breakout comes

After a breakout, the test (BUEC or LPSY) usually goes back to an operating level of the range you just left. The first one is the edge of the value area nearest to price: the VAH after a break up, the VAL after a break down. The second is the VPOC[pp.216–217]. That gives your Lesson 8 entry a price to wait at.

Who is in control

“We will always favor trading in the direction of the last high trading node generated.” In a rally, pull a profile over the move. While price stays above the last HVN (not necessarily the VPOC), only look for longs. A short scenario only appears once price crosses below that HVN[pp.218–220]. The more operating levels sit on your side of price, the better[p.220].

A Spring that can’t get back in

Context beats any single level. A potential Spring that gets back above the VAL and the weekly VWAP is a long scenario. One that can’t get back above the range low, the VAL or the VWAP is “a sign of significant weakness”: treat the range as distribution[pp.222–223].

P and b shapes

A P profile has a thin stem and a fat top. Buyers pushed price up easily, then a range formed at the top. It comes before re-accumulation or distribution. A b profile is the mirror: a fast fall, then a range at the bottom, which comes before accumulation or redistribution[pp.210–213]. Both are Phases A–B seen as a profile[p.209]. Read the order of events, not just the outline. The tradeable kind is trend first, then the range, because the next move starts from that range[p.211].

The trade plan

Entry at an operating level (VAH/VAL, VPOC, VWAP), with your trigger bar. Stop beyond a zone of earlier rejection, which is an LVN. Target at an HVN, because old balance pulls price toward it[p.235].
