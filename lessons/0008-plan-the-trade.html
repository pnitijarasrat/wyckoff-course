Plan the trade: zone, entry, stop, target

Seven lessons of reading charts lead up to this one: deciding where to get in, where you’re wrong, where to get out, and how many shares to buy. The book’s answer is short and mechanical. Today you’ll make it a habit.

Your win today

Given a labelled range, name the trading zone you’re in (Phase C, D or E). Then place a stop where the scenario is proven wrong and a target at a liquidity zone, check the R:R, and size the position to risk 1%. The result is three orders you could send to your broker.

Why this matters to you

Your mission ends with “a concrete entry, stop loss and take-profit for a trade, the way the book’s case studies do.” Everything so far was preparation for “the critical moment of every trader: the final decision making”[Classic p.208]. Lesson 7 ended with a lean, for example “lean re-accumulation, and wrong if it closes below $78.50.” That “wrong if” line is already half a stop loss. Today you’ll write the rest of the plan.

1 · Context first, then structure, then zone

The book makes every trading decision from three things, in this order: the context, the structure and the trading zone[p.191].

  • Context is what’s to the left of the chart, on your timeframe and a higher one. The rule: “trade in favor of the larger structure.” If you read accumulation, you only look for buys, and “discarding outright the sell trades”[pp.192–193]. You may miss a move, but you won’t be on the wrong side of it.
  • Structure is your read of the range, using Lessons 4–7.
  • Zones are where large traders have to trade, because that’s where the orders are. They open positions on shakeouts and need another pool of orders to close them. Those pools act like “price magnets”[pp.196–197]. That idea is the basis for your targets below.

2 · The three trading zones

The book allows trades in only three places[p.198]. The trade-off between them: “the sooner we get the signals, the greater the potential travel and the lower the reliability.”

ZoneEntries (accumulation; flip for distribution)ForAgainst
Phase CThe Spring itself, only if it came on low volume. The Spring’s Test is “one of every Wyckoff trader’s favorite” entries, and it must hold above the Spring low.Best R:R, because you’re at the edge of the rangeLeast confirmation, since the range is still young
Phase DA significant bar or a minor LPS on the way up through the range. The breakout test (BUEC/LPS) was Wyckoff’s own favourite.The whole structure is on your leftLess room left to the target
Phase EA significant bar, a pullback, or a small re-accumulation on a lower timeframe, in the trend outside the range.The “most ‘secure’” tradesThe least room left

Sources: Phase C pp.199–201, Phase D pp.201–204, Phase E pp.204–207. Why the Spring has to be light: high volume “tend[s] to be tested”, so wait for the test, which “normally… will be able to give us an even better Risk:Reward ratio” (pp.199–200). The book also lists an LPS entry for Phase C, but you can only see it after the breakout, so it’s “in many cases inoperable” (p.201).

3 · The trigger: a significant bar

Being in the zone isn’t enough. You also wait for proof that someone is acting there: a significant bar, which is the SOS bar or SOW bar from Lesson 6. For a bullish one, the book lists four features[pp.208–209]:

  1. A wider range than the bars before it.
  2. Higher volume, in line with that range.
  3. A close in the upper half of the bar.
  4. A close above a recent resistance.

Flip all four for a bearish bar. If no single bar qualifies, two ordinary bars together can[p.209]. Your entry is the close of the trigger bar.

4 · The stop: where the scenario is wrong

“Place the Stop Loss at the point where, if reached, the proposed scenario would be invalidated”. In general that means beyond the significant bar and beyond the whole scenario[pp.216–217]:

EntryLong stop (flip for shorts)
Directly into the SpringBelow the Spring low
The Spring’s TestBelow the SOS bar and the Test (tighter), or below the Spring low (wider)
A minor structureBelow the low of that minor structure
The breakout test (BUEC/LPS)Below the SOS bar and below the broken Creek

Sources: pp.217–220. For a breakout test, the book also allows a wider stop at the middle of the range (p.131). The book doesn’t use trailing stops, because they leave price too little room to move (pp.220–221). Stocks can gap past a stop overnight, so a stop doesn’t guarantee your exit price. That’s one more reason to keep each trade’s risk small.

5 · The target: where the orders are

The book replaces the old point-and-figure count with three exits[pp.221–226]:

  • Liquidity zones. These are earlier highs and lows, including the edges of the structure itself (the SC low, the AR high, the BC high). After a Spring test, the first target is the Creek[p.130]. Take targets from the higher timeframe too, and move them closer if price makes a new swing nearer to you[pp.224–225].
  • A climactic bar against you, or a completed Phase A (PS, SC, AR and ST, or their distribution versions) on your timeframe. Either one means the trend you’re riding has probably stopped. Take profit[pp.222–223].
  • Volume levels (VPOC, VWAP) come in Lesson 11.

6 · Size it, and send all three orders together

The book risks a fixed percentage of the account on every trade, and no more than 1%[p.214]:

shares = (account × 1%) ÷ (entry − stop)
R:R = (target − entry) ÷ (entry − stop). Skip trades where it’s below 1 (p.214).

Then send the entry, the stop and the target at the same time. If you place only the entry and price goes against you, you may talk yourself into moving the stop. “The end result in most cases will be a higher than expected loss”[pp.212–213].

Real charts · Do it in Deepvue (~25 min)

  1. Take the stock you scored in Lesson 7, or your live BUEC candidate from Lesson 6. Any watchlist stock in Phase C, D or E will also do.
  2. Context: check the weekly chart. Which side is allowed: longs only, shorts only, or nothing yet?
  3. Zone and trigger: which phase is it in, and which entry from the table does that allow? Has a significant bar printed yet? If not, write down the bar you’re waiting for.
  4. Stop: put it where the scenario fails, using the stop table above.
  5. Target: find the nearest liquidity zone on the daily chart and on the weekly chart. Work out the R:R. If it’s below 1, the trade is off.
  6. Size: use 1% of your real account, or a $25,000 one if you’d rather not share it.
  7. Set price alerts at the range edges. The book points out that you don’t need to watch a range all day, because a shakeout happens at the edges[p.200].
