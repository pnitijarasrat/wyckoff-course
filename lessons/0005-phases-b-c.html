Phases B and C: the cause and the shakeout

Phase A gave you a box. Now you’ll learn what happens inside it, and how to spot the false break that usually comes before the real move.

Your win today

On a range that has finished Phase A, label the Phase B tests (UA, ST as SOW, UT, mSOW), spot the Phase C shakeout (Spring or UTAD) and its test, and decide whether a break below support is a Spring you can act on, one you should wait on, or a real breakdown.

Why this matters to you

Phase C is where the book finds the best trade in the whole structure. A genuine test after the shakeout gives “the best risk/benefit ratio; since if the test is genuine, we will be very close to the end of the structure (where the Stop Loss order should be placed)”[Classic p.129]. Your stop goes just beyond the shakeout, and the target is the other side of the range. You’ll do the full trade plan in Lesson 8. Today is about recognising the setup.

It also protects you as a trader who already uses breakouts. A Spring is a breakdown that traps sellers, and a UTAD is a breakout that traps buyers. Both are built to catch the breakout traders and the breakout robots, whose orders give large traders the other side of their positions[pp.139–140].

Phase B: building the cause

After the ST, the market is in balance. Price rotates between the two Phase A limits while large traders “absorb most of the stock they need”[p.182]. Most of a range is Phase B, and the book wants it to last longer than Phases A and C[p.183].

Your job in Phase B is to watch the tests at each end. When price pokes slightly outside the box and comes back, that’s a test. It has a different name depending on which end it tests and which way you currently lean[pp.126–128]:

Phase B testIf you lean accumulationIf you lean distributionWhat it hints
Pokes above the top, comes backUA Upthrust ActionUT UpthrustAggressive buyers are present. It’s strength unless what follows says otherwise
Slips below the bottom, comes backST as SOW Secondary Test as Sign of WeaknessmSOW minor Sign of WeaknessSellers are urgent. The area will probably be tested again

The action is the same and only the label changes. If you’re unsure which way the range leans, the book’s shortcut is to call either one “ST in B”[p.128]. Each new extreme becomes the new edge of the box, which is where you then watch for the breakout or the shakeout[p.126].

The Phase B tests set up your Phase C bias
  • A UA (strength at the top) means a later break of the bottom is more likely a Spring than a real breakdown[p.141].
  • An mSOW (weakness at the bottom) means a later break of the top is more likely a UTAD than a real breakout[p.141].
  • If price tests one end and then can’t even reach the other end, that’s a structural failure. After a UA plus a failure to reach the lows, expect the breakout to start from the middle (an LPS) with no Spring[2.0 pp.76–77].

Phase C: the shakeout

Phase C is one event and its test. Large traders push price through the most obvious stop zone, beyond all the earlier lows (Spring) or highs (UTAD), to check that nobody is left on the other side[pp.184–185]. If the break gets no follow-through, price snaps back inside the range.

Three kinds of Spring, by how much supply shows up

TypeLooks likeWhat to do
#1 Terminal ShakeoutDeep break, wide bars, heavy volume. Demand must come in hard to reverse itWait. It must be tested before price can rise[pp.145, 149]
#2Moderate break, with volume and bar size both rising. Some supply is still aroundWait for the test. Expect more than one[pp.146, 149]
#3Slight break, narrow bars, light volume. No one wants to sell lower“A very powerful Spring on which you can directly take purchase positions”[p.147]

A good test of the Spring has narrower bars and less volume, and it holds above the Spring low. A test that fails these checks means more testing is coming[p.149]. The UTAD is the mirror image. It usually comes on moderate or heavy volume, and its test should stall below the UTAD high on less volume. Sometimes there’s no test at all, because supply floods in straight away[pp.150, 152].

A Spring is only a Spring once the range breaks

“Spring must necessarily cause the range to break up. Anything other than this should not be labeled as Spring. It will simply be a test.”[p.142] In real time, call it a potential Spring. At minimum, the shakeout should send price to the opposite end of the range[2.0 pp.78, 80–81]. If a potential Spring can’t produce even a small rally showing strength and then makes a new low, relabel it as a plain test[2.0 p.67].

Don’t force it

  • The shakeout may not reach the extremes. Strong buyers sometimes won’t let price get back to the lows. Then the Phase C test is just a higher low (an LPS), and the setup is harder to trade[pp.40–41, 185].
  • The real break and the shakeout can look the same on the break bar. The difference is what happens next: re-entry into the range means a shakeout, while acceptance outside the range means a real break[pp.141–142].
  • Re-label without ego. “Labels are not really important; what is important is the action behind them”[2.0 p.67].

The Phase B–C read

  1. Box: start from the Phase A range (climax and AR). Widen it whenever a Phase B test sets a new extreme.
  2. Phase B tests: mark each poke outside the box and the re-entry. Name it by end and lean, or just call it “ST in B”.
  3. Bias: a UA favours a Spring later; an mSOW favours a UTAD. A structural failure means don’t wait for a shakeout.
  4. Shakeout: when price breaks the box, watch the next few bars. Did it re-enter quickly? On what volume, and how deep did it go?
  5. Test: for a Spring #1 or #2, wait for a quieter higher low that holds above the Spring. For a #3, the Spring itself can be the trigger.

Real charts · Do it in Deepvue (~25 min)

  1. Take the ranges you used for Lesson 4. Pick the one that has gone sideways the longest, and add one more range of at least 2–3 months that has already broken out in either direction. Breakouts from bases are common on a trader’s watchlist, so you probably have some already.
  2. Draw the Phase A box (climax and AR). Then mark every Phase B test where price poked out and came back. Label each one UA, UT, ST as SOW or mSOW, or “ST in B” if you can’t tell.
  3. On the range that has broken out, look for the Phase C shakeout before the break. Was it a Spring/UTAD beyond all the earlier extremes, or a higher low/lower high that never reached them (an LPS/LPSY)?
  4. Grade it: how deep did it go, and how big was its volume relative to the 50-day average? Which Spring type was it? Was there a test, and did it hold?

Primary source: read this next (~25 min)

Villahermosa, The Wyckoff Methodology in Depth (your Classic Wyckoff.pdf). Read “The Secondary Tests of Phase B” (PDF pp.125–128), then Chapter 18, “Shaking” (pp.131–153), the most important chapter so far. The author calls the shakeout “the most important event that can occur in the financial markets.” Finish with the one-page summaries of Phase B (pp.182–183) and Phase C (pp.184–185).

Second book: Villahermosa, Wyckoff 2.0, PDF p.67 (re-labelling a potential Spring that fails), pp.75–77 (Phase B tests and structural failure) and pp.77–81 (the shakeout as the dominant clue, and why it must reach the opposite end). Cross-check: Bogomazov & Lipsett, “The Wyckoff Method: A Tutorial” (StockCharts ChartSchool). It covers Phases B and C, the Spring, the UTAD and the “test” of each in the same terms.
