Re-accumulation or distribution? Weighing the evidence

A stock you own has run up and gone sideways. Is the range a pause before the next leg, or the top? The two start out looking identical. This lesson teaches you to add up the evidence, one trace at a time, and to give more weight to the latest trace.

Your win today

Given a range after an uptrend, read the four footprints (the ST, the Phase B tests, the Phase C shakeout and the range’s character) as strength or weakness. Then make a call: lean bullish, lean bearish, or no lean yet. The same read works after a downtrend, to tell accumulation from redistribution.

Why this matters to you

This is the call behind “do I hold, add, or sell?” Both ranges start the same way, by stopping an uptrend. The book calls telling them apart “one of the most compromising situations that any Wyckoff trader will encounter”[Classic p.79]. The mirror case is just as hard. After a fall, telling accumulation from redistribution is “undoubtedly one of the most difficult tasks for the Wyckoff trader”[p.87]. Bruce Fraser adds the practical warning: re-accumulations “can take months to form”, so traders often sell them too early[Fraser 2022].

Same start, different job

RangeComes afterLeads toStarts out looking like
Accumulationa downtrendmarkupredistribution
Redistributiona downtrendmore markdownaccumulation

Re-accumulationan uptrendmore markupdistribution

A re-accumulation is “exactly identical to the accumulation process”. The only difference is that it starts by stopping an upmove[p.77]. Why does a healthy uptrend need one? As a trend runs, stock passes from strong hands to weak hands, and the large traders have to absorb it again before price can go further[p.78]. The more stock that is in weak hands, the longer the range takes[p.78]. Fraser says it in one line: “Distribution begins with the same characteristic footprint as Reaccumulation”[Fraser 2015]. In both, you see a PSY, a BC, an AR and an ST. Phase A alone won’t give you the answer.

You can’t know. You can only lean.

“In real time it is not possible to know what it is really about; whether it is about accumulation or distribution.” You only know for sure once the effect has played out, and by then it’s too late to trade[2.0 p.72]. That’s why every call in this lesson is a potential one. You add up the traces in favour of one side or the other[2.0 p.74].

The four footprints

Wyckoff 2.0 lists the traces in the order they appear[2.0 pp.73–82]. Split the range in half with a midline before you start.

FootprintStrengthWeakness
1 · The ST in Phase AIt ends in the upper half, or above the topIt ends in the lower half, or below the bottom
2 · The Phase B testsPrice tests the top of the rangePrice tests the bottom of the range
3 · The Phase C shakeoutA Spring below the lows that re-enters the rangeA UTAD above the highs that re-enters the range
4 · Phase D price and volumeWide up bars and rising volume after the shakeoutWide down bars and rising volume after the shakeout

A few notes on reading them:

  • The AR is also a clue. An AR that is short and overlapping, with no volume peak, shows no real intent from the other side. After a fall, that makes you “think of a process of redistribution”. After a rise, a weak AR plus an ST that goes above the BC makes you suspect re-accumulation[pp.113–114].
  • A Phase B test can be read two ways. A poke above the top may have cleared out the short sellers’ stops, or it may have been used to sell. What happens next tells you which[2.0 p.76]. If price then can’t reach the other end, that’s the structural failure from Lesson 5. It points to a breakout from the middle of the range, with no shakeout[2.0 p.77].
  • The shakeout outweighs everything before it. It is “the dominant event”, and “we will always give a higher relevance to the latest information available to us”[2.0 p.78]. Earlier traces still count, but a clear Spring or UTAD can overturn them.
  • No shakeout doesn’t mean no signal. In a weak distribution, the Phase C test is often just a quiet lower high, an LPSY, with no UTAD[2.0 p.77]. Fraser reads rallies “with diminishing price and volume characteristics” into a lower high as distribution[Fraser 2022].

The range’s character

The book also lists the features of each kind of range. They show up in the bars and volume through Phases B and C[pp.76, 84]:

Accumulation / re-accumulationDistribution / redistribution
Volume and volatilityShrink as the range developsStay high and constant, with wide swings
Tests of the far sideTests of the top on low volumeTests of the bottom on low volume
Wider, smoother movesUp movesDown moves
Late in the rangeRising highs and lowsFalling highs and lows

Redistribution “remains volatile during and at the end of its development”[p.87]. In an uptrend, a sharp drop that shakes out a re-accumulation without much of a range before it is an Ordinary Shakeout[p.148].

The read

  1. Name both candidates. If the range comes after a rise, it’s a potential re-accumulation or a potential distribution. If it comes after a fall, it’s accumulation or redistribution.
  2. Mark Phase A (BC or SC, AR, ST) and draw the range’s top, bottom and midline.
  3. Score each trace as it appears: strength, weakness, or no clear sign.
  4. Let the latest trace weigh most. A clear Phase C shakeout outweighs the earlier traces.
  5. Call it and name what would change your mind. For example: “Lean re-accumulation. A heavy drop through the AR low that stays down would make me wrong.” Lesson 8 turns each call into a trade plan.

Real charts · Do it in Deepvue (~25 min)

  1. Find a stock on your watchlist (ideally one you own) that rallied and has been ranging for at least four weeks.
  2. Mark the BC (and any PSY), the AR and the ST. Draw the range top, bottom and midline.
  3. Score the traces so far: where the ST ended, which end the Phase B tests went to, and any Phase C shakeout. For the range’s character, compare its volume with the 50-day average, and check whether the up swings or the down swings are wider.
  4. Make the call (lean re-accumulation, lean distribution, or no lean yet), and write down the one price action that would prove you wrong.
  5. Mirror: do the same for one stock that fell and is now ranging. Is it accumulation or redistribution?

Primary source: read this next (~30 min)

Villahermosa, Wyckoff 2.0 (your Wyckoff 2.0.pdf), §2.3 “How to distinguish between accumulation and distribution?” (PDF pp.72–82). It’s the clearest summary of the four footprints, written by the author of the book this course is aiming at. Then, in The Wyckoff Methodology in Depth (Classic Wyckoff.pdf), read Chapters 10–13 on accumulation, re-accumulation, distribution and redistribution (PDF pp.74–88). They’re short and mostly cover the “common characteristics” lists. Finish with “The implications of its development” (pp.113–114) on what the AR tells you.

Second book: Villahermosa, Wyckoff 2.0, PDF pp.72–82 (§2.3: real-time uncertainty, ST type in Phase A, Phase B tests and reaction, the shakeout as the dominant event, Phase D price and volume).

Cross-check: Bruce Fraser, “Distribution or Re-Accumulation?” (StockCharts, Mar 2022), a live example with a second LPSY as the tell. Also “Take the Fork in the Road” (StockCharts, Aug 2015), on how both ranges start with the same footprint.
